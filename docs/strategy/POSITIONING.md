### The operating reality this document is written against

`STATED BY PXTO` — supplied 2026-08-28.

> PXTO is a **new company** with a **limited number of publicly presentable
> projects**.

This is the single most important input to the strategy. It is not a weakness to
be disguised — it is a constraint that determines which positions are available.

Positions that require volume, history, scale or named clients are **closed**.
Positions built on clarity of thinking, quality of execution and honesty of
claim are **open**, and a new company can occupy them immediately.

The PRD already anticipates this: build credibility "sem exagerar experiência ou
inventar resultados" (§2.5). This document treats that as a strategic
instruction, not a compliance note.

**Nothing in this document invents a client, a number, a result, a testimonial,
a partner, a certification, an award, a team size or a company history.**

## 1. Target audience

`DERIVED` — PRD §8.

### 1.1 Who decides

The site addresses **decision-makers in operations and technology**, not
technical implementers (§8.1):

- Business owners, partners, directors
- Operations managers
- Commercial managers
- Administrative managers
- Professionals responsible for processes

These people share a characteristic that shapes everything: they **own a
problem, not a technology budget line**. They are evaluated on whether the
operation works, not on the stack that runs it.

### 1.2 Company situations that generate demand

`DERIVED` — §8.1.

- Companies running **multiple systems** that do not talk to each other
- Companies with **manual processes** consuming staff time
- Companies needing a **specific solution built** that no product provides

### 1.3 Audience characteristics worth designing for

`INTERPRETED` — from §8 and §13.

| Characteristic | Consequence for positioning |
| --- | --- |
| Not technical, but technically literate | Explain substance, never jargon (§7) |
| Time-poor; scanning, not reading | The problem must be recognised in seconds (§13) |
| Has been disappointed by a vendor before | Overclaiming actively costs credibility (§14.5, Brand Foundation) |
| Describes friction, not service categories | Entry points are problem-shaped (§14.7, Brand Foundation) |
| Carries internal risk if the choice fails | Reassurance matters more than excitement |

The last row deserves emphasis. This buyer is not looking for the most exciting
vendor. They are looking for the one **least likely to waste their time**. For a
new company, that is a favourable frame: it is earned by how you communicate,
not by how long you have existed.

### 1.4 Who is explicitly not the audience

`INTERPRETED` — from §8, §4.

- Developers and technical buyers evaluating a stack
- Companies shopping purely on price for a template website
- Enterprises requiring formal vendor qualification, procurement processes,
  compliance certifications or references at scale — a new company cannot
  satisfy these, and pursuing them wastes effort

## 2. Ideal customer profile

`PROPOSAL` — the PRD defines audience by role and situation only (§8). Sector,
size, geography and ticket are **undefined** (Brand Foundation §15.7). The
profile below is a working hypothesis to be confirmed, adjusted or rejected.

### 2.1 Firmographic dimensions — status

| Dimension | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Sector / vertical | **Undefined.** PRD is horizontal by design |
| Company size | **Undefined** |
| Revenue | **Undefined** |
| Geography | **Undefined.** PRD is Portuguese-language throughout; no market stated |
| Deal size | **Undefined** |
| Buying process | **Undefined** |

These must not be asserted anywhere until PXTO decides them.

### 2.2 Working ICP hypothesis

`PROPOSAL`

> A company large enough to have accumulated **several systems and real
> operational processes**, but not large enough to have an internal development
> team that can absorb the work.

That band is where the problem exists and no internal solution does. Below it,
the company has one tool and no integration problem. Above it, an internal team
or an incumbent vendor already owns the work.

### 2.3 Qualifying signals

`INTERPRETED` — from §8.2. A prospect is a strong fit when:

- Two or more systems hold overlapping data that a person reconciles by hand
- A recurring process depends on someone remembering to do it
- Spreadsheets act as the connective tissue between real systems
- An off-the-shelf product covers 80% of a need and the last 20% is the problem
- Someone internally has already said "we should automate this"
- There is a named owner of the problem with authority to start

### 2.4 Disqualifying signals

`INTERPRETED`

- Wants the cheapest possible website with no operational component
- Wants staff augmentation or a body to manage, not a solution
- Requires vendor certifications, references at scale, or formal procurement
- Wants to buy a specific named technology rather than solve a problem
  (conflicts with §4 and §57 — PXTO chooses the technology for the problem)
- No internal owner for the problem

### 2.5 Best-fit entry point

`INTERPRETED`

The strongest first engagement is a **contained, well-bounded problem with a
visible outcome** — one integration, one automated process, one focused
application. For a company with limited public proof, a small delivered success
is the fastest route to a larger second engagement, and it produces the case
study the portfolio needs (§14.2, Brand Foundation).

## 3. Customer problems

`DERIVED` — PRD §13, §8.2.

The PRD's framing:

> Sua empresa já tem tecnologia. O problema é quando ela não conversa.

This is a strong problem statement because it is **non-accusatory**. It does not
tell the buyer they made bad decisions. It tells them the situation is
structural — which it usually is.

### 3.1 The problem categories

`DERIVED` — §13.

| Problem | What it looks like inside the company |
| --- | --- |
| **Disconnected systems** | Two tools that both hold the customer, neither authoritative |
| **Manual processes** | Work that exists only because nothing connects |
| **Duplicated data** | The same information entered more than once |
| **Scattered information** | No one place answers a basic question |
| **Repetitive tasks** | Skilled people doing mechanical work |
| **Tools that do not fit** | A product that solves the general case, not this one |

### 3.2 The underlying condition

`INTERPRETED`

Each of the six is a symptom of the same condition: **the company's operation
has outgrown the tools it assembled**. Systems were bought individually to solve
individual problems, and no one owned the space between them.

That space — between the systems, between the tools, between the process and
what the software actually does — is where PXTO works. It is the strategic
centre of the positioning (§14).

## 4. Customer jobs-to-be-done

`INTERPRETED` — from §8.2, expressed as jobs.

### 4.1 Functional jobs

The buyer is hiring PXTO to:

- **Make two systems share information** without a person in the middle
- **Remove a recurring manual step** from a process that runs constantly
- **Get a specific capability** that no product on the market provides
- **Establish or replace a digital presence** that actually functions
- **Turn an idea into something that works** and can be shown or used

### 4.2 The job behind the job

`INTERPRETED`

Functionally, the buyer wants systems connected. Underneath, they want:

> **To stop having this problem, permanently, without becoming responsible for
> managing the solution.**

The distinction matters. Selling "integration" sells a project. Selling "this
problem stops being yours" sells an outcome — and it is what PRD §15's
*Evoluímos* step implies.

### 4.3 Emotional and social jobs

`INTERPRETED` — inferred from buyer role, not from any PXTO claim.

| Job | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Reduce personal risk** | Choose a vendor that will not fail visibly |
| **Stop being the bottleneck** | The problem is often blocking the buyer specifically |
| **Look competent internally** | The buyer proposed this; it needs to work |
| **Regain capacity** | Free the team for work that requires judgement |
| **Trust the explanation** | Understand what is being built without being technical |

The last is under-served in this market and is directly addressable by a new
company through communication quality alone.

### 4.4 The hiring moment

`INTERPRETED`

Buyers rarely act on chronic friction. They act on a trigger: growth that makes
manual work untenable, a new system that must connect to old ones, a person
leaving who "was the process", an error that cost something visible, or a
deadline that exposes the gap.

**Consequence:** the site is read by people already in a trigger moment. It does
not need to create urgency — it needs to be legible and credible fast, and to
make contact frictionless (§3, §28).

## 5. Pain points

`DERIVED` / `INTERPRETED` — from §8.2, §13. Grouped by who feels them.

### 5.1 Operational pains
Time lost to manual reconciliation · errors from re-entry · processes that
depend on one person's memory · work that cannot scale without adding headcount ·
delays waiting for information to move between systems.

### 5.2 Informational pains
No single reliable answer to a basic question · reporting assembled by hand ·
decisions made on stale data · information that exists but is unreachable.

### 5.3 Strategic pains
The operation cannot grow without proportional headcount · the company has paid
for tools whose value it is not extracting · a competitive capability is blocked
by a technical gap · an idea cannot be tested because nothing can be built.

### 5.4 Buying pains — the pains PXTO's own positioning must address

`INTERPRETED`

These are frictions in choosing a vendor, and they are where a new company can
differentiate immediately, because they cost nothing but discipline:

| Buying pain | How positioning answers it |
| --- | --- |
| "I don't know what I need, so I can't brief it" | Lead with problems, not service menus (§14.7, Brand Foundation) |
| "Every vendor says the same thing" | Restraint and specificity are the contrast (§14.5, Brand Foundation) |
| "I can't tell who is actually capable" | Show work and reasoning, not adjectives (§16) |
| "The last vendor disappeared after delivery" | *Evoluímos* is part of the process (§15) |
| "I'll be locked into their technology" | Technology-agnostic positioning (§57) |
| "It'll be over-engineered and over-priced" | Build only what is necessary (§32, §52) |

## 6. Desired outcomes

`INTERPRETED` — from §2, §5, §8.2, §15.

What "success" means to the buyer, in their own terms:

### 6.1 Immediate
- The manual step is gone
- The systems exchange information without supervision
- The thing that did not exist now exists and works

### 6.2 Operational
- The team's time returns to work requiring judgement
- Information is consistent across systems
- The process runs whether or not anyone remembers it
- Volume can grow without proportional headcount

### 6.3 Strategic
- The operation stops constraining the business
- Existing tools deliver the value they were bought for
- The company can build what it needs rather than adapting to what exists
- The solution can evolve as needs change, without a rebuild (§15, §50.15)

### 6.4 Relational
- The buyer understands what was built and why
- There is someone to call when something changes
- The choice does not need to be defended internally

**Constraint:** these are the outcomes PXTO works toward. They must **never** be
presented as achieved results, quantified savings or guaranteed effects unless
PXTO supplies evidence for a specific project (§53). See §13.

## 7. PXTO value proposition

`DERIVED` — PRD §5, extended structurally.

### 7.1 Core statement

> **A PXTO transforma necessidades reais de negócio em soluções digitais,
> conectando sistemas, automatizando processos e desenvolvendo software sob
> medida.**

### 7.2 The structure of the proposition

`INTERPRETED`

The proposition has three layers, and they must be kept in this order:

| Layer | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **What the buyer gets** | The operational problem stops existing |
| **How PXTO does it** | Integrating, automating, building, publishing — whichever the problem requires |
| **Why it holds** | The technology is chosen for the problem, not the reverse (§57) |

Most competitors lead with layer 2. Leading with layer 1 is the differentiating
choice, and it is available to a company of any age.

### 7.3 Capability-level propositions

`DERIVED` — §23–§26.

| Capability | Proposition |
| --- | --- |
| **Integrações** | *Faça seus sistemas conversarem.* |
| **Automação** | *Automatize o trabalho que não deveria precisar ser manual.* |
| **Software** | *Quando uma solução pronta não basta, construímos a sua.* |
| **Sites** | *Presença digital construída para funcionar.* |

Note the shape: each is a **problem resolved**, none is a technology named. This
is the pattern any future capability must follow.

### 7.4 What the proposition deliberately does not claim

`INTERPRETED`

Not: cheapest, fastest, biggest, most experienced, most innovative, or best.
None is evidenceable, and each is claimed by everyone (§7, §53).

## 8. Positioning statement

`PROPOSAL` — internal strategic instrument, not copy. Requires approval.

**Full form:**

> For **operations and business leaders whose systems and processes have
> outgrown the tools they assembled**,
> **PXTO** is a **technology company that works in the space between systems** —
> connecting what does not connect, automating what should not be manual, and
> building what does not exist.
> Unlike **agencies, software houses, consultancies and automation platforms**,
> which each own one piece of the problem,
> PXTO **starts from the operational problem and builds only what it actually
> requires**, using whichever technology fits — because the tools change and the
> capability does not.

**Short internal form:**

> PXTO resolve o problema que está entre os sistemas.

**Notes on construction:**

- The frame of reference is a **problem space**, not a service category. This is
  what allows integrations, automation, software, AI-enabled solutions and
  websites to coexist without any one defining the company (§4, §57).
- The differentiation clause makes **no comparative quality claim** — it
  describes a structural difference in approach, which requires no evidence of
  scale to be true.
- Nothing in the statement depends on how long PXTO has existed.

**This is not website copy.** It is the instrument copy is written against.

## 9. Competitive alternatives

`INTERPRETED` — from §4's exclusion list and §8.2's buyer needs.

The buyer is not choosing between PXTO and a named competitor. They are choosing
between **ways of resolving the problem**. No specific companies are named here;
none has been researched, and inventing a competitive landscape would violate
§53.

| Alternative | What it offers | Where it falls short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Do nothing / absorb it manually** | Zero cost, zero risk | Cost is real but invisible; scales badly. **The most common competitor** |
| **Internal workaround** — spreadsheets, a technical employee | Immediate, free, controlled | Fragile, undocumented, dependent on one person |
| **Hire internally** | Permanent capacity | Slow, expensive, hard to justify for one problem |
| **Freelancer** | Cheap, fast to start | Continuity risk; narrow capability; no ownership after delivery |
| **Traditional / creative agency** | Strong visual craft, brand work | Weak on systems and operations; §4 and §26 place PXTO outside this |
| **Software house / dev shop** | Real engineering capacity | Executes specification; typically does not diagnose the operational problem |
| **Consultancy** | Diagnosis and process design | Recommends; often does not build |
| **iPaaS / no-code automation platforms** | Fast, low cost, self-service | The company must own the build and maintain it; breaks on non-standard cases |
| **The incumbent vendor's own services** | Knows their product | Solves inside their product only; no incentive to integrate outward |

### 9.1 The strategic reading

`INTERPRETED`

Two observations shape the position:

**First, the real competitor is inertia.** The buyer has lived with this problem
for months or years. The decision is usually "act or continue", not "PXTO or
someone else". Positioning must therefore make the **cost of the current state**
visible — which §13's problem section does — rather than argue superiority over
rivals.

**Second, the alternatives are each partial.** The consultancy diagnoses but
does not build. The software house builds but does not diagnose. The platform
automates but must be owned by the client. The agency makes it look right but
does not connect it. **The gap is the whole path — from understanding the
problem to something that runs.** That gap is PXTO's territory (§14), and
occupying it requires coherence, not scale.

## 11. Differentiators

`INTERPRETED`

A differentiator must be **true, relevant to the buyer, and hard to copy
credibly**. Marketing adjectives fail all three.

### 11.1 Structural — true now, independent of history

**1. Coverage of the whole problem, not one slice.**
Connect, automate, build, publish — under one party. The alternatives in §9 are
each partial. This is a structural fact about the capability set (§1.1), not a
quality claim.

**2. Technology-agnostic by policy.**
No stack to defend, no platform to resell, no partner margin to protect. The
tool is chosen for the problem (§57). Most alternatives cannot say this
honestly, because their economics prevent it.

**3. Problem-first method.**
*Entendemos → Desenhamos → Construímos → Integramos → Evoluímos* (§15). The
company does not begin with technology. This is verifiable in the first meeting,
which makes it credible without any track record.

**4. Disciplined restraint.**
No invented proof, no superlatives, no borrowed authority (§7, §53). In a
category where inflation is the norm, refusing it is a signal — and one that is
**hard to copy**, because copying it requires giving up claims competitors
depend on (§14.5, Brand Foundation).

**5. Difficult technical range, already demonstrated.**
The two documented projects involve AI, computer vision, image generation and
conversational interfaces (§17, §18) — capabilities most agencies and
freelancers do not hold. Subject to disclosure clearance (§20). Must be framed
by problem, not by technology (§14.3, Brand Foundation).

### 11.2 Candidate — require PXTO confirmation before use

`PROPOSAL` — each implies an operating-model fact not established anywhere.

- **Direct access to whoever builds the solution** — implies an operating model
  and, indirectly, a team size. Cannot be claimed until confirmed
- **Speed of engagement** — plausible for a small company, but a claim about
  delivery time requires evidence
- **Flexible commercial model** — undefined
- **Ongoing support after delivery** — implied by *Evoluímos* (§15), but the
  actual service commitment is undefined

### 11.3 Explicitly not differentiators

Quality, professionalism, dedication, modern technology, "we listen to our
clients", "we treat every project as unique". Every competitor claims these.
Claiming them costs credibility (§7).

## 13. Proof points that should NOT be claimed

`DERIVED` — §7, §53, plus the new-company reality.

**Absolutely prohibited** — no exceptions, no softened phrasing:

### 13.1 Volume and history
Number of clients · years of operation · number of projects delivered ·
"experiência consolidada" · "atendemos empresas de diversos setores" · anything
implying a client base.

### 13.2 Scale and structure
Team size · "nossa equipe de especialistas" · offices · presence in multiple
markets · capacity claims.

### 13.3 Borrowed authority
Client logos without authorisation (§17.1, §20) · testimonials · case studies
not cleared · partner or vendor badges · certifications · awards · press ·
"trusted by" of any kind.

### 13.4 Metrics and results
Percentage savings · hours saved · ROI · efficiency gains · uptime or SLA
figures · performance figures for client systems · any metric not authorised by
the client (§20).

### 13.5 Superlatives and comparatives
Best · leading · reference · pioneer · specialist in [sector] · most complete ·
market-leading — none is evidenceable (§7).

### 13.6 Implied claims — the most likely failure mode

`INTERPRETED`

The prohibitions above are easy to observe literally and easy to violate by
implication. Watch for:

| Construction | What it implies |
| --- | --- |
| Plural "nossos clientes", "nossos projetos" | A volume that does not exist |
| A generic logo strip or placeholder grid | Clients that do not exist |
| "Já ajudamos empresas a…" | An accumulated track record |
| Invented statistics as decoration | Measured results |
| Stock photos of a team | A team of that size |
| "Desde 2024" without a confirmed date | A verifiable history |
| Generic testimonial cards, even unfilled | Social proof by layout alone |

**Design implication:** the site's layout must not contain slots that only make
sense when filled with social proof. An empty testimonial section is worse than
no testimonial section, and a layout that visibly wants a logo strip creates
pressure to invent one.

### 13.7 The rule

> If PXTO cannot point to the source of a claim, the claim does not go on the
> site — regardless of how ordinary it seems in the category (§53).

## 14. Strategic positioning territory

`PROPOSAL` — the PRD says what PXTO is not (§4) but never names the space it
occupies. This section proposes an answer to the open question recorded in
Brand Foundation §15.8.

### 14.1 Selection criteria

Any viable territory must:

1. Be **technology-agnostic** — survive a change of stack (§57)
2. Hold all four capabilities **as peers**, plus AI, without one dominating
   (§4, §14.1 Brand Foundation)
3. Be **claimable without accumulated proof** — available to a new company
4. Be **occupiable** — not already saturated by the alternatives in §9
5. **Accommodate future growth** into new capabilities without repositioning

### 14.2 Territories considered

| Territory | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **The integration specialist** | Sharp and memorable, but collapses the four capabilities into one and violates §4. Rejected |
| **The AI company** | Fits current portfolio; explicitly prohibited (§4) and creates dependency on one technology. Rejected |
| **The bespoke development partner** | Accurate but crowded; every software house claims it. Does not hold Sites well. Weak |
| **Speed / access — "enterprise capability without the overhead"** | Attractive, but rests on claims about delivery time that cannot be evidenced (§11.2). Not available now |
| **The space between systems — applied engineering for real operations** | Meets all five criteria. **Recommended** |

### 14.3 Recommended territory

> ### Engenharia aplicada à operação
> **The space between the systems.**

The territory is defined by a **problem**, not a technology and not a service
category. PXTO owns the space where a company's tools, processes and actual
needs fail to meet — and builds whatever closes that gap.

**Why it satisfies each criterion:**

| Criterion | How |
| --- | --- |
| Technology-agnostic | The territory is a problem space. Any technology that closes the gap belongs — including AI, and including technologies that do not exist yet |
| Four capabilities as peers | Integração connects the space · Automação removes the manual work inside it · Software builds what was missing from it · Sites is the operational surface where the company meets the outside. None is the identity |
| Claimable without proof | It is a claim about **where PXTO works**, not about how much it has done |
| Occupiable | §9 shows every alternative owns one slice. Nobody owns the whole path from diagnosis to running system |
| Accommodates growth | Any future capability — data, agents, internal platforms, whatever follows — is admissible if it closes an operational gap |

**Where Sites fits.** Sites is the capability most at risk of pulling the brand
toward "agency" (§9, §11.1 risk). Inside this territory it is coherent: the site
is **presença digital construída para funcionar** (§26) — a functioning
operational surface, integrated with the company's tools, not a visual artefact.
Positioned this way, Sites reinforces the territory instead of diluting it.

**Where AI fits.** AI is one of the technologies used to close the gap — exactly
the placement §57 gives it ("Integrações, automações, software, IA e sites são
ferramentas dentro dessa proposta"). Under this territory, PXTO can do
substantial AI work and grow that work **without becoming an AI company**,
because the territory is defined by the problem, not the tool. This directly
addresses the risk in §15.1 below.

### 14.4 The territory in one line

Internal use only, not copy:

> PXTO trabalha no espaço entre os sistemas — onde a operação real acontece e
> nenhuma ferramenta pronta chega.

### 14.5 What this territory forbids

Accepting it means declining:

- Purely visual website work with no operational component
- Reselling or implementing a single platform as the core offer
- Positioning any one technology, AI included, as the company's identity
- Staff augmentation

## 15. Positioning risks

`INTERPRETED`

Ordered by likelihood × impact. Each carries a mitigation.

### 15.1 AI gravity — **highest risk**
Both available projects are AI-categorised (§17, §18) while §4 forbids AI
positioning. The portfolio is the load-bearing proof (§14.2, Brand Foundation),
so the proof currently contradicts the position.
**Mitigation:** frame projects by problem solved, not technology; keep AI
vocabulary out of hero, navigation and metadata; prioritise a non-AI case
(§12.3). Tracked in Brand Foundation §15.5.

### 15.2 Breadth read as vagueness
Four capabilities plus "we solve problems" can read as "we do everything",
which reads as "we specialise in nothing". This is the standing cost of
refusing a narrow position.
**Mitigation:** the territory (§14) supplies the specificity that the capability
list cannot. Specificity comes from **problem description**, not from narrowing
the offer. If §13's problem section is generic, the whole position collapses.

### 15.3 The proof gap
A new company with an uncleared portfolio asks the buyer to take the claim on
faith.
**Mitigation:** shift the proof burden onto what can be shown now (§12.2) — the
site's own execution, the precision of the problem description, and the
reasoning visible in project pages. Clear the two projects urgently.

### 15.4 Restraint misread as smallness
Deliberate understatement (§7) can read as tentativeness or lack of ambition,
particularly to a buyer accustomed to inflated vendor language.
**Mitigation:** restraint must be paired with **confidence and precision**, not
hedging (Brand Foundation §6). Design carries the authority the copy declines to
claim (§33, §14.6 Brand Foundation). Never apologise for being new; never
mention it either.

### 15.5 Sites commoditising the brand
Website work attracts price-shopping buyers and pulls perception toward
"agency", against §4 and §26.
**Mitigation:** position Sites as an operational surface within the territory
(§14.3); disqualify pure-template buyers (§2.4).

### 15.6 Portfolio drift
Whatever PXTO sells next becomes what PXTO appears to be. A run of similar
projects silently repositions the company.
**Mitigation:** treat portfolio composition as a positioning decision. Review
the mix whenever a project is added; deliberately seek cases that fill gaps.

### 15.7 Corporate brand without visible substance
The brand speaks as a company (§1.1) while presenting no team, no history and no
client list. A buyer may read "company" as a facade over one person.
**Mitigation:** substance is demonstrated through work and thinking, not
personnel. Depends on the unresolved founder-visibility decision (Brand
Foundation §15.6) — an unaddressed gap invites the worst interpretation.

### 15.8 Category ambiguity
If the buyer cannot file PXTO mentally, they may not recall it when the need
arises — a referral and memory problem more than a conversion problem.
**Mitigation:** §14's territory gives a memorable frame that is not a category
label. Accept the trade-off consciously.

### 15.9 Buyer risk aversion toward new vendors
The buyer carries internal risk (§1.3) and a new supplier increases it.
**Mitigation:** reduce commitment size at entry (§2.5); make the process legible
(§15); be exact about what will happen. Never counter with claims that
compensate for newness (§13).

### 15.10 Positioning–execution divergence
The position claims problem-first diagnosis and disciplined restraint. If
delivery does not work that way, the positioning becomes a liability rather than
an asset — the gap is visible to every client.
**Mitigation:** this is an operational commitment, not a communications one. The
positioning must be adopted by the company, not just by the website.

## 16. Positioning principles

`DERIVED` / `INTERPRETED` — operating rules. Every content, design and
commercial decision should be checkable against these.

1. **The problem is the position.** PXTO is defined by the space it works in,
   not by the services it sells or the technologies it uses (§14, §57).

2. **The four capabilities are peers, permanently.** No single area becomes the
   identity — not the most recent work, not the most fashionable, not the
   easiest to explain (§4).

3. **Technology is a means, never an identity.** Any technology, AI included, is
   admissible and none is the position. The stack may change; the position does
   not (§57).

4. **Never claim what cannot be sourced.** Where evidence does not exist, the
   claim does not exist. This applies to implication as strictly as to statement
   (§53, §13.6).

5. **Demonstrate rather than assert.** Capability is shown through work,
   reasoning and the quality of the site itself — not through adjectives (§16,
   §12.2).

6. **Restraint is the differentiator.** It works only if total; a single
   inflated claim invites the reader to discount everything else (§14.5, Brand
   Foundation).

7. **Speak to the problem owner, not the technologist.** Business language,
   operational framing, no jargon as credential (§7, §8).

8. **Newness is neither hidden nor advertised.** Do not reference how long PXTO
   has existed, in either direction. The position does not depend on it.

9. **Compete against inertia first.** The primary alternative is the buyer doing
   nothing. Make the cost of the current state visible before arguing capability
   (§9.1, §13).

10. **The company speaks, not a person.** One voice, every surface (§1.1, §27).

11. **Portfolio composition is a positioning instrument.** What is published
    defines the position more strongly than any statement about it (§15.6).

12. **Build only what the problem requires.** Restraint in the work is the same
    value as restraint in the claim (§32, §52).
